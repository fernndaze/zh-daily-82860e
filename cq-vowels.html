The e-vowel splits in two

Std's single e-sound becomes two in CQ: a rounded o in the ge-class words, and an open 'e' (like the e in 'bet') in the entering-tone set. Your drilled dé-words carry that second vowel — we keep spelling them 'dé' to match your cards, but the sound leans toward 'dê'.

gē → gō (rhymes with 'or')stdCQ

Brother — the rounded o family (also hē 'drink', hé 'river', gè 'measure word').

bái → bé (e as in 'bet')stdCQ

White — the entering-tone e, bright and open.

hēi → hêstdCQ

Black.

gěi → gêstdCQ

To give.

gēge → gō gōstdCQ

Older brother doubled — the rounded vowel plus a full-tone second syllable in one word.

Nasal finals regroup — but an/ang survives

There is no eng and no ing in the city accent. And — the anti-overgeneralization lesson — an and ang stay DISTINCT.

The mergers that are real

The control pair

sān vs sāng — STILL differentstdCQ

Anti-overgeneralization #2: an/ang does NOT merge in CQ. Both words stay distinct — don't flatten them.

Why the rumor exists: CQ's 'an' vowel is backed (drawn toward the 'a' in 'father'), so untrained OUTSIDER ears mishear it as ang. The contrast is intact — a dedicated Yuzhong study confirms minimal pairs like 'ship' vs 'bed' stay clearly apart. Trust your training over the meme.

The CQ ending-system (not Beijing's -r)

Beijing fuses '-r' into the previous syllable and swallows half of it. Chongqing attaches its ending syllable to a FULL word base — so words keep one more audible syllable than their Beijing cousins. Around ten distinct ending finals exist (vs Chengdu's ~4).

huǒguōr → huǒ gūrstdCQ

Hotpot — the ending fuses INTO the final syllable (the o is absorbed), but the word stays two beats, not Beijing's crushed one.

háizi vs wárstdCQ

'Child': std uses one word, CQ uses wár — different WORD, different sound system.

standard: háizi
tāngyuárstdCQ

Glutinous rice ball — the ending is obligatory here (also dìwár 'young guy').

hāhārstdCQ

In a moment — CQ's own ending word, absent from std entirely.

yì dīdīrstdCQ

A teeny bit — reduplicated base plus ending.

Where Beijing hears endings everywhere, CQ uses them almost only on two-syllable bases — Beijing-style one-syllable endings ('moment', 'thing' with fused r) simply don't exist in city speech. Your lint rule (never write fused -r forms) matches the grammar of the accent.

Rhythm: nothing gets swallowed

The city core has essentially no neutral tone. Every suffix, every particle, every second syllable of 'māma' carries its full tone. Result: an even, staccato, drumbeat rhythm — fast and steep, which locals frame as river-port directness (the fierce sound is prosody, not anger).

māma → mā māstdCQ

Mom — both syllables equal weight.

xiézi → hái zístdCQ

Shoes — old reading PLUS the full-tone 'zī' ending.

dōngxi → dōng xīstdCQ

Thing — std swallows the second syllable; CQ pronounces it whole.

xuésheng → xió sēnstdCQ

Student — the capstone: io vowel + flat s + fronted nasal + full final tone. Four shifts, two syllables.
